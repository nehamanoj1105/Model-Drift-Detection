\title{Regime-Aware Policy Transfer for Computationally\\ Efficient Adaptation under Network Data Drift}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Paper ID: \textit{under double-blind review}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Network telemetry is non-stationary, and predictive models trained on an earlier
period degrade when operating conditions change. Conventional adaptation responds by
retraining, effectively but at the repeated full cost of rebuilding a model. We ask
whether adaptation policies learned for previously observed operating regimes can be
reused instead of rebuilt. We present Regime-Aware Policy Transfer (RAPT), which
associates a learned ensemble policy with each regime, retrieves it when a matching
regime recurs, and refreshes it when monitoring shows the stored policy no longer
fits. Evaluated against frozen, event-driven and full-retraining baselines on four
network streams under a strictly prequential protocol, RAPT reduced adaptation CPU
relative to full retraining by 53--88\%, but its predictive performance was not
uniformly better: it recorded lower macro-F1 on all four streams, and on one stream
blind reuse was actively harmful, a failure a larger novelty-refit buffer repairs. On
the 5G Campus ablation stream a cost-aware configuration matched full retraining
within measurement noise ($\Delta$macro-F1 $=-0.0022$, $p=0.44$) at roughly 23\% lower
adaptation cost. Two
mechanisms were rejected on measured evidence: a relative-degradation trigger that
cannot fire on persistently poor policies, and an incremental learner dominated on
both accuracy and cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
concept drift, network telemetry, online learning, adaptive ensembles,

\section{Introduction}

Operational network data rarely holds still. Load, radio conditions, subscriber
mobility and configuration changes all shift the distribution of the measurements
that monitoring systems observe, and a classifier fitted to one period of a 5G
testbed or an ISP netflow archive will not stay accurate indefinitely. The standard
remedy is to retrain when performance falls. That works, but it is paid for
repeatedly, and on high-rate telemetry the cumulative rebuild cost can exceed the
cost of serving predictions; lightweight detection-and-adaptation frameworks have
been proposed specifically for constrained devices \cite{9427288}.

Many effective detectors exist for \emph{when} to adapt
\cite{bifet2007learning,page1954continuous,gama2004learning,baena2006early,ross2012exponentially,dos2016fast,brockhoff2020time},
but none exploits the structure of the change. Network conditions are
frequently \emph{recurring} rather than merely novel: a weekday congestion profile,
a radio configuration, or an operator-specific QoS regime may appear, disappear, and
return. Recurring concepts motivated some of the earliest work in the field
\cite{schlimmer1986incremental,widmer1994combining}, yet the common response remains
to relearn, discarding a model that is often still the right one.

This paper studies whether that information can be retained and reused. RAPT
maintains a repository of adaptation policies indexed by operating regime. At a
regime boundary it checks whether a policy for the incoming regime is already
stored; if so, the stored ensemble is retrieved at near-zero adaptation cost, and
otherwise a policy is trained and stored. Because a retrieved policy may no longer
fit if the regime's label relationship has changed, RAPT also monitors its
streaming accuracy and can refresh it. RAPT changes when training is paid for, not
how the ensemble is trained.

Our answer to whether the substitution is safe is conditional. Where regimes recur
with stable label semantics, reuse preserves accuracy while removing most of the
adaptation cost; where regimes recur but the feature-to-label relationship has
shifted, reuse retrieves a stale policy and degrades performance, and the
enhancement that repairs it is what makes the approach viable there. The
contributions are a regime-conditioned policy-transfer mechanism that stores and
retrieves ensemble policies by regime; a monitoring and refresh strategy whose
cost-aware configuration matches full-retraining accuracy on the evaluated stream at a
fraction of its cost;
an evaluation across four network streams under one prequential protocol against four
established drift detectors; and a failure analysis of harmful blind reuse, a trigger
that cannot fire by construction, and an incremental learner dominated on both axes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Concept and Data Drift}
Distributional change in streaming data is separated into covariate shift, where
$P(X)$ changes while $P(Y\mid X)$ is stable, and concept drift, where $P(Y\mid X)$
itself changes \cite{gama2014survey,lu2018learning}. Survey work documents a long
history of taxonomies and adaptation strategies \cite{khamassi2018discussion},
beginning with the recurring concepts of STAGGER \cite{schlimmer1986incremental} and
the robustness--flexibility trade-off studied by Widmer \cite{widmer1994combining}.
The two call for different responses: covariate shift can be absorbed by
recalibrating a decision boundary, whereas concept drift invalidates the learned
mapping. Recurring drift is common in systems whose load follows periodic patterns
yet has received less attention than gradual or abrupt drift \cite{gomes2017adaptive}.

\subsection{Drift Detection}
Detectors trigger adaptation rather than performing it. ADWIN compares sub-window
means over an adaptive window \cite{bifet2007learning}; Page-Hinkley is a sequential
cumulative-sum procedure \cite{page1954continuous}; DDM and EDD monitor the error rate
and its variance \cite{gama2004learning,baena2006early}, while RDDM adds reduced false
alarms \cite{barros2017rddm} and an ECDD-EWMA control chart tracks an exponentially
weighted moving average of the error \cite{ross2012exponentially}. Label-free detectors test the feature stream instead,
using Hoeffding bounds \cite{frias2014online}, an incremental Kolmogorov--Smirnov
statistic \cite{dos2016fast}, or the earth-mover distance \cite{brockhoff2020time},
with lightweight variants for constrained IoT deployments \cite{9427288}. They
differ in sensitivity and firing frequency, which translates into adaptation cost.

\subsection{Adaptive and Online Learning}
Online ensembles adapt by updating constituent models as data arrives. Dynamic
weighted majority weights learners by recent accuracy and adds or removes them as the
stream evolves \cite{JMLR:v8:kolter07a}, an idea disagreement-minimizing methods had
already applied to drifting concepts \cite{helmbold1994tracking}; adaptive random
forests extend online bagging with drift-aware tree replacement \cite{gomes2017adaptive},
the accuracy-updated ensemble reweights components under drift \cite{brzezinski2013reacting},
and such designs have been specialised to social big-data and IoT streams
\cite{abbasi2021elstream,yang2021pwpae}. Single-pass incremental learners such as
Hoeffding trees bound memory and update cost per example \cite{domingos2000mining},
and the River library implements the detectors and incremental learners used here
\cite{montiel2021river}. Incremental updating changes \emph{how} a model is updated
rather than whether an already-adequate model can be retrieved; Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
reports a comparison in which it did not improve the accuracy--cost trade-off here.

\subsection{Transfer under Recurring Conditions}
Transfer learning moves representations or parameters from a source task to a
related target task \cite{pan2010transfer}. Retaining rather than discarding learned
structure has an older lineage in work on the role of forgetting \cite{markovitch1988role}
and the cost of reusing compiled knowledge chunks \cite{tambe1988some}, both
observing that relearning what is already known is wasteful. RAPT transfers something
narrower: a previously fitted policy and its regime identity are stored and retrieved
whole, so the decision is not how to adapt but whether adaptation is needed at all.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let a stream be a chronological sequence $x_1, x_2, \dots$, where
$x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a feature vector and $y_t \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ its label,
available only after the prediction for $x_t$. The stream is partitioned into
fixed-size windows $W_1, \dots, W_T$, and window $W_k$ carries a regime identifier
$r_k \in \mathcal{R}$. A regime is a semantically defined operating condition
rather than a cluster label, such as a network scenario or a time-of-day block. A
\emph{policy} $\pi$ is a fitted model with its decision rule, and the repository is a
mapping $\mathcal{P}: \mathcal{R} \rightarrow \pi$ populated as regimes are
encountered. When the stream moves from $r_{k-1}$ to $r_k$, the controller chooses
$a_k \in \{\textsc{reuse}, \textsc{train}\}$: reuse $\mathcal{P}(r_k)$ if it exists, or
train from the recent buffer. With $M(\pi, W)$ a performance measure (macro-F1 here)
and $c(\pi)$ the adaptation CPU cost of producing $\pi$, the problem is to keep
$M(\cdot, W_k)$ close to a freshly trained policy's while minimizing $\sum_k c(a_k)$.
Reuse assumes $P(Y \mid X, r_k)$ is unchanged since the policy was stored; if the
regime identifier recurs but the conditional relationship has shifted, the stale
policy is applied with no signal that it is stale. Distinguishing recurrence of the
regime from recurrence of the label relationship is the central difficulty.

\section{Proposed Method: RAPT}

\subsection{Architecture and Repository}
RAPT sits between the stream and the model and makes one decision per regime
boundary. Each window is predicted with the currently active policy and evaluated
once its labels are available, and the policy changes only at a regime transition or
when monitoring reports degradation --- the prequential discipline of
Section~\ref{sec:protocol}, which prevents a window's labels from influencing its own
prediction. A regime is represented by its semantic identifier rather than a learned
clustering, which keeps repository keys stable and avoids the failure mode in which
clustering splits one operating condition into several keys and defeats reuse; the
mechanism therefore depends on a usable regime signal, an assumption we revisit in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}. The repository stores, per regime, a checkpoint
holding the fitted ensemble, its soft-voting weights, and creation and last-access
timestamps. Checkpoints are immutable, so reuse retrieves the ensemble by reference
rather than copying it, and only the small weight vector is adjusted when calibration
applies; retrieval cost is therefore independent of model size, making reuse cheap
relative to retraining.

\subsection{Matching and Transfer}
Matching is by regime identifier: on a transition to regime $r$ the controller tests
whether $r \in \mathcal{P}$. In the enhanced variant a fingerprint-similarity gate
also requires the current window's feature summary to be close to the fingerprint
recorded at checkpoint creation, converting ``has this regime been seen?'' into the
weaker ``has a sufficiently similar instance been seen?'' and letting the mechanism
decline reuse when a regime recurs under visibly different conditions. On reuse the
retrieved ensemble becomes the active policy and its soft-voting weights may be
recalibrated on the most recent buffered window by measuring the recent accuracy of
the two components and renormalizing them; calibration is bounded to the last 50
observations and skipped below 20, so its cost is fixed and small. When no checkpoint
exists a new policy is trained from a class-anchored buffer and stored; that buffer
retains a stratified slice of the training prefix, applied identically to every
adaptive model here, to prevent a refit from collapsing onto one class when the recent
window is homogeneous.

\subsection{Monitoring, Refresh, and Fallback}
Two monitoring rules are available, and their difference is one finding of this work.
The \emph{absolute} rule fires when rolling accuracy falls below a fixed floor; the
\emph{relative} rule fires when accuracy has dropped by more than a fixed margin
against a decayed reference built from that policy's earlier accuracy.
Section~\ref{sec:trigger} shows the consequence: a policy that was never good cannot
be detected by a relative rule, because the reference decays toward the poor level and
the gap the rule looks for never opens. A refresh rebuilds the active policy from the
recent buffer and replaces its checkpoint, so later reuses retrieve the refreshed
policy. The default refresh refits the 100-tree ensemble on a 1000-observation buffer;
the cost-aware refresh keeps the trigger but refits 20 trees on a 300-observation
buffer (Section~\ref{sec:cost}).

RAPT-Enhanced adds \emph{buffer-blended novelty refitting}, refitting a novel regime
on a 1500-observation buffer rather than 500, and \emph{selective parity refitting},
refitting a reused policy and replacing its checkpoint when rolling accuracy falls
below a parity threshold over a short window. Parity refitting addresses the
stale-reuse failure directly, since it does not require the regime identifier to
change before a retrieved policy can be corrected. When no checkpoint exists and the
similarity gate declines an available one, the controller trains a fresh policy and
stores it under the incoming regime identifier; this fallback is exactly the
full-retraining behaviour RAPT avoids only when unnecessary.


\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Datasets}
Four network streams are used, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.
\textbf{5G Campus Network QoS} is a per-window QoS stream from a 5G campus testbed
with 13 features and a three-class target (GOOD, DEGRADED, BAD) from the next
window's 90th-percentile delay; regimes are scenario labels and three recur 14 times.
\textbf{UGR'16} is an ISP netflow archive of per-minute aggregates with 133 features
\cite{ugr16}; its calibration split holds only blacklisted traffic with no labelled
attacks, so the labelled 30-day block is used, with a binary target and
workday/weekend crossed with four-hour time-of-day blocks giving twelve regimes and
168 recurrence events, the strongest recurrence structure here. \textbf{NordicDat}
is a set of cross-border LTE and 5G predictive-QoS traces \cite{nordicdat} with a
three-class next-window median-delay target and mobile-operator regimes, the weakest
and fewest-transition stream. \textbf{5G NR End-to-End Latency} is a simulated dataset
\cite{nrlatency} of 499 windows of 500 packets spanning four regimes ($ABCDACBDAB$)
with the campus QoS target, used for the natural-drift baseline and controlled drift
experiments.

\subsection{Preprocessing, Ordering, and Leakage Control}
All streams follow one lightweight pipeline: order by timestamp, drop identifier and
constant columns, keep numeric features, map infinities to missing, drop rows with
missing targets, impute with training-prefix medians, encode categorical targets
deterministically, and window. No PCA, feature selection, hyper-parameter search or
global normalization is applied, and target thresholds and scaling statistics are
computed on the initial training prefix only. Streams are never shuffled. The prefix
is 20\% of the windows (36 for the three cross-dataset streams, 100 for 5G NR), is
never evaluated as test data, and is the only source of target thresholds. Windows
are predicted before their labels are used for adaptation, giving the prequential
order \textbf{predict $\rightarrow$ evaluate $\rightarrow$ adapt}; regime annotations
are read from the stream, not derived from the target.

\subsection{Models and Detectors}
The five primary models share one base architecture and one retraining buffer, so
differences reflect only the adaptation mechanism: a heterogeneous soft-voting
ensemble of a random forest and an extra trees classifier, each 50 trees of depth 7. \emph{Frozen} trains once on the prefix and never
adapts; \emph{Event-Driven} retrains when window accuracy drops below a fixed
threshold; \emph{Full Retraining} rebuilds at every regime transition; \emph{RAPT}
and \emph{RAPT-Enhanced} are the controllers of Section~IV.

ADWIN, Page-Hinkley, EDDM and ECDD-EWMA drive the same retraining harness with
identical model, buffer, capacity and minimum retrain interval, so only the trigger
differs. Parameters are fixed a priori and not tuned on the test stream (ADWIN
$\delta=0.002$; Page-Hinkley minimum 30 instances, $\delta=0.005$; EDDM $\alpha=0.95$,
$\beta=0.9$; ECDD-EWMA $\lambda=0.2$, $L=2$). As ground-truth boundaries are unavailable, we report
detected events, adaptation cost and predictive performance rather than detector
precision and recall.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}
Predictive performance is reported as macro-F1, accuracy, precision and recall.
Adaptation cost is reported as \emph{adaptation CPU}, the time inside adaptation
routines only, and \emph{runtime}, the total streaming time; these are kept separate
because a reduction in adaptation time does not translate proportionally into total
runtime. We also record retraining and reuse events and trees trained or reused. All
experiments use seeds $[42, 43, 44, 45, 46]$ and report mean $\pm$ standard
deviation; paired comparisons use the Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Cohen's $d$.
Window pairing has 143--146 observations, seed pairing $n=5$ and a smallest attainable
two-sided $p$-value of $0.0625$; that ceiling is stated wherever it binds, and
non-significant results are reported as a failure to establish a difference rather
than as equivalence. Measurements used a single process with \texttt{n\_jobs}$=1$,
so timings are comparable within the study but not portable. All four streams are
evaluated by one harness under the configuration of Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

On 5G Campus all five models are close to saturation: Full Retraining recorded the
highest macro-F1 ($0.9739 \pm 0.0049$), and RAPT and RAPT-Enhanced both recorded
$0.9402 \pm 0.0028$, $0.0338$ below it and significant in that direction
($p = 0.0625$, $d_z = -13.05$); the two variants agree to four decimals, so
the enhancement had no measurable effect here. UGR'16 is where the
mechanism's assumptions break down and is the most informative result: Frozen
recorded the highest macro-F1 ($0.9688 \pm 0.0038$), consistent with attacks being
sparse and clustered, while RAPT recorded $0.8503 \pm 0.0346$, $0.1088$ below Full
Retraining ($p = 0.0625$, $d_z = -3.16$) and the lowest of the five
models despite 132 reuses. The twelve regimes recur and their identifiers stay valid
across the 30-day block, but the relationship between netflow features and attack
labels does not, so reuse retrieves policies matching the regime label but not its
label semantics. RAPT-Enhanced recovered to $0.8988 \pm 0.0412$, a gain of $0.0485$
over RAPT ($p = 0.0625$, $d_z = 1.05$). An ablation shows this gain is attributable
to the larger novelty-refit buffer ($500 \to 1500$ observations), not to the parity
trigger: at its default threshold the parity refit fires zero times, and raising the
base novelty buffer to $1500$ alone reproduces $0.8988$.

NordicDat is the weakly predictable stream: Frozen recorded $0.3037 \pm 0.0182$
while adaptation helps, with Full Retraining at $0.4765 \pm 0.0171$ and RAPT at
$0.3978 \pm 0.0361$, a deficit of $0.0786$ that is significant at the seed level
($p = 0.0625$, $d_z = -4.04$); RAPT did record the highest accuracy here
($0.6037 \pm 0.0099$ against $0.5920 \pm 0.0077$), a precision-favouring decision
profile rather than better macro-F1. On 5G NR the five models span $0.1103$, with
Frozen highest ($0.9048 \pm 0.0056$) and RAPT $0.0642$ below Full Retraining. The
predictive results do not support a claim that RAPT improves accuracy: Full
Retraining records the highest macro-F1 on two of four streams and RAPT is below it
on all four, so the case rests on cost and on when reuse is safe.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Behaviour}
The adaptation-cost reduction and the predominance of reuse appear on all four
streams, so both are properties of the mechanism; the predictive outcome is not.
RAPT-Enhanced improves over base RAPT by $0.0485$ on UGR'16, $0.0666$ on NordicDat
and $0.0501$ on 5G NR and is identical on 5G Campus, so the enhancement matters only
where regimes recur and their label relationship changes. Reuse frequency alone says
nothing about whether a retrieved policy was appropriate: on UGR'16 both RAPT and
RAPT-Enhanced reuse 132 times, yet RAPT records the lowest macro-F1 of the five models
and RAPT-Enhanced $0.8988$, locating the failure in the reuse decision rather than in
reuse as such. The NordicDat orderings of accuracy and macro-F1 invert ($0.6037$
against $0.5920$), which cannot be resolved with the present data.

\subsection{Efficiency Ablation}
\label{sec:ablation}
Table~\ref{tab:ablation} reports the mechanism ladder on 5G Campus. RAPT-Full
recorded $0.9537 \pm 0.0064$ at $0.6211$\,s; replacing its trigger with a
relative-degradation one (RAPT-Evidence) fired \emph{zero} refreshes and left
macro-F1 at $0.9402 \pm 0.0028$; an absolute floor (RAPT-Floor) also fired zero
refreshes at $0.9402 \pm 0.0028$, because Campus window accuracy never falls below
the $0.60$ floor. The periodic schedule is what actually refreshes: with a full refit
every five windows it recorded $0.9745 \pm 0.0044$ at $4.6486$\,s (22 refreshes), and
with the cheaper refit RAPT-Cheap recorded $0.9717 \pm 0.0081$ at $0.9801$\,s against
Full Retraining's $0.9739 \pm 0.0049$ at $1.2779$\,s. The difference of $-0.0022$ is
not significant ($p = 0.4375$, $d_z = -0.34$), so it does not establish a
difference; relative to the full-refit periodic refresh it keeps accuracy while
cutting adaptation CPU by roughly 79\%. RAPT-Incremental ($0.7164$ at $0.1874$\,s) is
dominated on both axes. The supported statement is therefore \emph{matched} accuracy
at substantially lower cost, not superiority.

\subsection{Cost Drivers and Trigger Behaviour}
\label{sec:cost}
The cheap refresh was chosen from direct measurement. Varying one factor at a time on
the base ensemble, a 20-tree refit on a 1000-observation buffer cost $65.8$\,ms
against $320.1$\,ms for 100 trees on the same buffer, while holding tree count at 20
and growing the buffer from 200 to 1000 changed the cost from $37.3$ to $65.8$\,ms;
tree count therefore dominated buffer size. Table~\ref{tab:ablation} places the RAPT
variants on the accuracy--cost plane for this stream.

\label{sec:trigger}
The zero-refresh result for RAPT-Evidence (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) is
mechanism-level rather than a tuning outcome. A relative trigger compares current
accuracy against a decayed reference built from that policy's own past. When a regime
is persistently poor rather than deteriorating, the reference decays toward the poor
level and the gap the rule tests for never opens, so the trigger stays silent even
though the policy is unfit. On 5G Campus neither the relative rule nor the absolute
floor fires, because window accuracy never drops below the floor; on NordicDat and
5G~NR, where accuracy does dip, both rules fire, with the relative rule firing more
often ($31.4$ and $43.4$ refreshes against $28.2$ and $15.6$). The floor threshold is
stream-specific and would require calibration elsewhere.

\label{sec:detector}

Under the shared harness the reported counts are as follows. On the window error
signal (143--146 windows for the cross-dataset streams) every detector fires at most
twice: ADWIN, Page-Hinkley and ECDD-EWMA fire zero times on all four streams and

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
The paired comparisons support a narrow set of conclusions. Under seed-level pairing
($n=5$) RAPT's macro-F1 deficit against Full Retraining is significant at the floor
$p = 0.0625$ on all four streams, with paired effect sizes $d_z$ of $-13.05$ (Campus),
$-3.16$ (UGR'16), $-4.04$ (NordicDat) and $-4.58$ (5G~NR); the moving-block
bootstrap over windows agrees on all four. RAPT-Enhanced's recovery over RAPT is
likewise significant at $p = 0.0625$ on UGR'16 ($+0.0485$, $d_z = 1.05$), NordicDat
($+0.0666$, $d_z = 4.00$) and 5G~NR ($+0.0501$, $d_z = 5.44$) and is exactly zero on
5G Campus. The five-seed ablation comparisons are limited by power as well as effect
size: RAPT-Cheap's difference from Full Retraining ($-0.0022$, $p = 0.4375$,
$d_z = -0.34$) and from the full-refit periodic refresh ($-0.0027$, $p = 0.4375$)
are both non-significant, so they establish no difference rather than equivalence. One
caveat applies throughout: window-level tests treat windows from one stream as
independent, overstating the effective sample size.

Because five seeds cap the attainable two-sided $p$-value at $0.0625$, we repeated the
cheap configurations at twenty seeds (42--61) with a Holm correction. All eight
Holm-corrected comparisons are significant: RAPT-Cheap beats the cheap periodic refit
and the cheap full retrain on 5G Campus ($+0.0024$, $+0.0021$) and UGR'16 ($+0.1572$,
$+0.0921$), and loses on 5G NR ($-0.0195$, $-0.0116$) and NordicDat ($-0.0401$,
$-0.0588$), all with Holm-adjusted $p < 0.01$.

\subsection{Label-Delay Sensitivity}

Latency telemetry reveals a window's target only one window later. With that delay,
5G Campus is unchanged and 5G NR changes negligibly ($|\Delta| \le 0.0064$), but
NordicDat degrades for every adapting method, most for RAPT ($0.4618 \to 0.3362$,
$p = 0.0625$) and Full Retraining ($0.4806 \to 0.4205$); RAPT's NordicDat advantage
does not survive the delay.

\section{Discussion}

The cost result has a straightforward explanation. Retraining at a regime transition
rebuilds the ensemble from a buffer, at a cost scaling with tree count and buffer size,
whereas reuse retrieves an ensemble stored by reference for a dictionary lookup plus a
bounded weight calibration. Where regimes recur with stable label relationships the
rebuild is unnecessary, so the saving tracks how often regimes recur. RAPT-Cheap works
because tree count dominates refit cost, so a cheap refresh corrects a nearly adequate
policy, and the periodic schedule is what makes it effective, since a reused policy
would otherwise stay frozen for the whole regime.

The two rejected mechanisms failed for identifiable reasons. The incremental learner
recorded $0.7164$ macro-F1 at $0.1874$\,s, dominated on both axes by the batch
variants, and the batch ensemble is already near ceiling on a 13-feature, three-class
problem, so this says more about this stream than about incremental learning generally.
The trigger finding generalises more cleanly: a relative trigger compares a policy
against its own past, so a policy that was always poor has no relative drop to detect,
a structural blind spot rather than a tuning problem.

Recurrence is the condition under which the mechanism is worth deploying. The cost
saving appears wherever reuse occurs, but the accuracy penalty is concentrated on
UGR'16, where regimes recur while their label relationship does not: precisely where a
reused policy is stale. The repair we measured there came from a larger novelty-refit
buffer ($500 \to 1500$ observations), which lifted RAPT from $0.8503$ to $0.8988$,
rather than from the parity trigger, which fires zero times at its default threshold.
Reuse on the regime identifier alone is therefore unsafe when label semantics can
drift independently of it, and a stream without recurrence reduces to retraining with
extra bookkeeping.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

Five seeds cap the attainable two-sided $p$-value at $0.0625$, so several large-effect
comparisons remain formally non-significant and the ablation results are limited by
power; the twenty-seed comparisons are the exception. Window-level tests treat windows
from one stream as independent, overstating the effective sample size, and timings are
comparable within the study but not portable.

Three measurement caveats bound the quantitative claims. The detector ordering
reflects a window-level error signal and a retrain throttle; on a per-sample signal
ADWIN and Page-Hinkley do fire, so the comparison characterises a configuration
rather than the detectors. The 5G NR row is a 500-packet window whereas the Campus
stream is 1{,}790 samples over 179 ten-packet windows, so per-window resolution
differs across streams. The reported gain of RAPT-Enhanced over RAPT on UGR'16 is
confounded with the novelty-refit buffer size, which the parity trigger does not
control.

Further limitations follow from the design. The mechanism depends on regime
recurrence, which is measured rather than assumed; a stream without recurrence never
exercises the repository. The RAPT-Floor threshold is calibrated on the evaluated
stream and the 20-tree cheap refit is chosen for this ensemble, so both would need
re-examination where the pre-/post-drift difference is larger. The repository is never
pruned, and the approach assumes regime similarity is meaningful, which could be
questionable if the regime signal were noisy.

The controlled drift-severity study (severity definitions, evidence that the
concept-drift transformation changes $P(Y\mid X)$ rather than $P(X)$, and the
resulting curves) is reported in the supplementary material. Its concept-drift
isolation uses a boundary-only controller with no in-regime trigger, so the inertness
it records under regime-internal concept drift bounds that controller rather than the
monitored variant described here.



\section{Conclusion}

Retraining is the conventional drift remedy, and where conditions recur that
expenditure is partly redundant. RAPT retains each regime's policy and retrieves it
when the regime returns, retraining only when no suitable policy exists and refreshing
one that monitoring shows has stopped fitting. Across four network streams it reduced
adaptation CPU by 53--88\% versus full retraining but recorded lower macro-F1 on all
four, and where regimes recur with changed label relationships blind reuse was
harmful, recovering only with a larger novelty-refit buffer. Reuse is therefore worth
deploying where regimes recur with stable or monitored label semantics; the natural
extension is a reuse decision that tests whether a retrieved regime is still
semantically the same.
