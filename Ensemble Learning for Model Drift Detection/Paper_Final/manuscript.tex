\title{Regime-Aware Policy Transfer for Computationally\\ Efficient Adaptation under Network Data Drift}

\author{\IEEEauthorblockN{Anonymous Submission}
\IEEEauthorblockA{Paper ID: \textit{under double-blind review}}}

\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Network telemetry is non-stationary, and predictive models trained on an earlier
period degrade when operating conditions change. Conventional adaptation responds
by retraining, which is effective but repeatedly pays the full cost of rebuilding
a model. We examine whether adaptation policies learned for previously observed
operating regimes can be reused instead of rebuilt. We present Regime-Aware Policy
Transfer (RAPT), which associates a learned ensemble policy with each regime,
retrieves that policy when a matching regime recurs, and refreshes it when
monitoring indicates the stored policy no longer fits. RAPT is evaluated against
frozen, event-driven and full-retraining baselines on four network streams under a
strictly prequential protocol. Across the four streams RAPT reduced adaptation CPU
relative to full retraining by 48--92\%, but its predictive performance was not
uniformly better: it recorded lower macro-F1 on all four streams, and on one
stream blind reuse was actively harmful, a failure that a parity-refit mechanism
repairs. A cost-aware configuration matched full retraining within measurement
noise ($\Delta$macro-F1 $=+0.0022$, $p=0.31$) at roughly 40\% lower adaptation
cost. Two mechanisms were rejected on measured evidence: a relative-degradation
trigger that cannot fire on persistently poor policies, and an incremental learner
that was dominated on both accuracy and cost.
\end{abstract}

\begin{IEEEkeywords}
concept drift, network telemetry, online learning, adaptive ensembles,
regime-aware learning, computationally efficient adaptation

\section{Introduction}

Operational network data rarely holds still. Load, radio conditions, subscriber
mobility and configuration changes all shift the distribution of the measurements
that monitoring systems observe, and a classifier fitted to one period of a 5G
testbed or an ISP netflow archive will not stay accurate indefinitely. The standard
remedy is to retrain when performance falls. That works, but it is paid for
repeatedly, and on high-rate telemetry the cumulative rebuild cost can exceed the
cost of serving predictions. Keeping that cost within budget is a recurring concern
in streaming deployments, and lightweight detection-and-adaptation frameworks have
been proposed specifically for constrained devices \cite{9427288}.

The streaming-learning literature has produced effective detectors for \emph{when}
to adapt, including ADWIN \cite{bifet2007learning}, Page-Hinkley tests
\cite{page1954continuous}, error-rate methods such as DDM and EDD
\cite{gama2004learning,baena2006early}, and distributional tests based on
exponentially weighted statistics or distance measures
\cite{ross2012exponentially,dos2016fast,brockhoff2020time}. What these detectors do
not provide is a way to exploit the structure of the change. Network conditions are
frequently \emph{recurring} rather than merely novel: a weekday congestion profile,
a radio configuration, or an operator-specific QoS regime may appear, disappear, and
return. Recurring concepts motivated some of the earliest work in the field
\cite{schlimmer1986incremental,widmer1994combining}, yet the common response remains
to relearn. When a previously encountered regime returns, the model trained for it
is often still the right model, and rebuilding an equivalent one discards
information that was already paid for.

This paper studies whether that information can be retained and reused. RAPT
maintains a repository of adaptation policies indexed by operating regime. At a
regime boundary it checks whether a policy for the incoming regime is already
stored; if so, the stored ensemble is retrieved and used directly at near-zero
adaptation cost, and otherwise a policy is trained and added to the repository.
Because a retrieved policy may no longer fit if the regime's label relationship
has changed, RAPT also monitors the retrieved policy's streaming accuracy and can
refresh it.

The mechanism is deliberately narrow. RAPT is not a new classifier and does not
change how the ensemble is trained; it changes when that training is paid for. The
empirical question is whether the substitution is safe, and our answer is
conditional. Where regimes recur with stable label semantics, reuse preserves
accuracy while removing most of the adaptation cost; where regimes recur but the
feature-to-label relationship has shifted, reuse retrieves a stale policy and
degrades performance, and the enhancement that repairs it is what makes the
approach viable there. We report both outcomes, plus two mechanisms we implemented,
measured, and rejected.

The contributions are as follows. First, a regime-conditioned policy-transfer
mechanism that stores learned ensemble policies by regime and retrieves them on
recurrence, avoiding full retraining when a suitable prior policy exists. Second, a
monitoring and refresh strategy for detecting when a retrieved policy has stopped
fitting, with a cost-aware refresh configuration that retains the accuracy of a
full refit at a fraction of its cost. Third, an empirical evaluation across four
network streams under one prequential protocol, covering predictive performance,
adaptation cost, policy reuse, and the recurrence structure the mechanism depends
on, together with a comparison against four established drift detectors that shows
detector-driven adaptation and policy reuse trade accuracy against cost rather than
one dominating the other. Fourth, a failure analysis covering a case where blind
reuse is harmful, a trigger that cannot fire by construction, and an incremental
learner dominated on both axes.

\section{Related Work}

\subsection{Concept and Data Drift}
Distributional change in streaming data is conventionally separated into covariate
shift, where $P(X)$ changes while $P(Y\mid X)$ is stable, and concept drift, where
$P(Y\mid X)$ itself changes \cite{gama2014survey,lu2018learning}. Surveys of the
area document a long history of taxonomies and adaptation strategies
\cite{khamassi2018discussion}, beginning with the recurring concepts of the STAGGER
system \cite{schlimmer1986incremental} and the robustness--flexibility trade-off
studied by Widmer \cite{widmer1994combining}. The two call for different responses,
since covariate shift can often be absorbed by recalibrating a decision boundary
whereas concept drift invalidates the mapping the model learned. Recurring drift, in
which an earlier distribution reappears, has received less attention than gradual or
abrupt drift despite being common in systems whose load and configuration follow
periodic patterns \cite{gomes2017adaptive}.

\subsection{Drift Detection}
Detectors provide the trigger for adaptation rather than the adaptation itself.
ADWIN maintains an adaptive window over a stream and detects change by comparing
sub-window means \cite{bifet2007learning}. The Page-Hinkley test is a sequential
cumulative-sum procedure from industrial quality control \cite{page1954continuous}.
DDM and its successor EDD monitor the error rate and its variance, signalling when
the error distribution departs from its observed minimum
\cite{gama2004learning,baena2006early}, while RDDM reacts to the same statistics
with reduced false alarms \cite{barros2017rddm}. EDMA tracks an exponentially
weighted moving average of the error against its own dispersion, an idea also
exploited by EWMA-based control charts \cite{ross2012exponentially}.
Label-free detectors instead test the feature stream, using Hoeffding bounds
\cite{frias2014online}, an incremental Kolmogorov--Smirnov statistic
\cite{dos2016fast}, or the earth-mover distance \cite{brockhoff2020time}, and
lightweight versions of these tests have been adapted for constrained IoT
deployments \cite{9427288}. These detectors differ in sensitivity and in how often
they fire, and that difference translates directly into adaptation cost.

\subsection{Adaptive and Online Learning}
Online ensembles adapt by updating constituent models as data arrives. Dynamic
weighted majority weights its learners by recent accuracy and adds or removes them
as the stream evolves \cite{JMLR:v8:kolter07a}, an idea that
disagreement-minimizing methods had already applied to drifting concepts
\cite{helmbold1994tracking}. Online bagging and boosting approximate batch
resampling under a streaming constraint, and adaptive random forests extend this
with drift-aware tree replacement \cite{gomes2017adaptive}. Rebuilding component
weights in response to drift is the basis of the accuracy-updated ensemble
\cite{brzezinski2013reacting}, and ensemble designs of this kind have been
specialised to social big-data and IoT streams \cite{abbasi2021elstream,yang2021pwpae}.
Single-pass incremental learners such as Hoeffding trees bound memory and update
cost per example \cite{domingos2000mining}, and the River library provides
practical implementations of both the detectors and the incremental learners used
here \cite{montiel2021river}. Incremental updating is attractive because its
per-example cost is independent of accumulated history, but it is a different
mechanism from policy reuse: it changes \emph{how} a model is updated rather than
whether an already-adequate model can be retrieved. Section~\ref{sec:ablation}
reports a direct comparison, in which incremental updating did not improve the
accuracy--cost trade-off on the stream we evaluated.

\subsection{Transfer under Recurring Conditions}
Transfer learning moves representations or parameters from a source task to a
related target task \cite{pan2010transfer}, where the object transferred is model
structure or feature representation. Retaining rather than discarding previously
learned structure has an older lineage in work on the role of forgetting
\cite{markovitch1988role} and on the cost of reusing compiled knowledge chunks
\cite{tambe1988some}, both of which observe that relearning what has already been
learned is wasteful. RAPT transfers something narrower: a previously fitted policy,
together with the regime identity under which it was fitted, is stored and later
retrieved as a whole. The decision RAPT makes is not
how to adapt a model but whether adaptation is needed at all, given that a
candidate policy already exists. Where recurrence is absent the mechanism
degenerates to ordinary retraining, as we observe on the stream with the fewest
regime transitions.

\section{Problem Formulation}

Let a stream be a sequence of observations in chronological order,
$x_1, x_2, \dots$, where $x_t \in \mathbb{R}^d$ is a feature vector and
$y_t \in \{1,\dots,C\}$ its label, available after the prediction for $x_t$ has been
made. The stream is partitioned into consecutive fixed-size windows
$W_1, \dots, W_T$, and window $W_k$ carries a regime identifier
$r_k \in \mathcal{R}$. A regime is a semantically defined operating condition rather
than a cluster label: a network scenario for the 5G testbed stream, a
workday/weekend and time-of-day block for the netflow stream, and a mobile network
operator for the cross-border trace.

A \emph{policy} $\pi$ is a fitted predictive model with its decision rule, and the
policy repository is a mapping $\mathcal{P}: \mathcal{R} \rightarrow \pi$ populated
as regimes are encountered. When the stream moves from $r_{k-1}$ to $r_k$, the
controller chooses an action $a_k \in \{\textsc{reuse}, \textsc{train}\}$: reuse
$\mathcal{P}(r_k)$ if it exists, or train a new policy from the recent buffer. With
$M(\pi, W)$ a performance measure (macro-F1 here) and $c(\pi)$ the cost of
producing $\pi$ in adaptation CPU time, the problem is to keep $M(\cdot, W_k)$ close
to what a freshly trained policy would achieve while minimizing $\sum_k c(a_k)$.

The difficulty is that reuse is appropriate only while the stored policy still fits
the regime. Reuse of $\mathcal{P}(r_k)$ assumes $P(Y \mid X, r_k)$ is unchanged since
the policy was stored. If the regime identifier recurs but the conditional
relationship has shifted, that assumption fails and reuse is silently harmful,
because the retrieved policy is applied without any signal that it is stale.
Distinguishing recurrence of the regime from recurrence of the label relationship is
the central difficulty the controller must handle.

\section{Proposed Method: RAPT}

Fig.~\ref{fig:rapt_methodology} shows the overall data flow, from window
construction through regime matching to policy reuse or fallback.

\subsection{Streaming Architecture}
RAPT sits between the stream and the predictive model and makes one decision per
regime boundary. Observations arrive in order; each window is predicted with the
currently active policy and evaluated once its labels are available. The active
policy changes only at a regime transition or when monitoring reports degradation.
This ordering, predict then evaluate then adapt, is the prequential discipline
described in Section~\ref{sec:protocol} and is what prevents a window's labels
from influencing its own prediction.

\subsection{Regime Representation}
A regime is represented by its identifier, derived from the semantic annotation
carried by the stream. Using the semantic label rather than a learned clustering
keeps the repository keys stable and interpretable, and avoids the failure mode in
which a clustering algorithm splits one operating condition into several keys and
defeats reuse. The consequence is that the mechanism depends on the stream
providing a usable regime signal, an assumption we return to in
Section~\ref{sec:limitations}.

\subsection{Policy Repository}
The repository stores, for each regime identifier, a checkpoint containing the
fitted heterogeneous ensemble, its soft-voting weights, and bookkeeping fields
recording when the checkpoint was created and last accessed. Checkpoints are
immutable, so a reuse retrieves the stored ensemble by reference rather than
copying it, and only the small weight vector is adjusted when calibration applies.
Retrieval cost is therefore independent of model size, which is what makes reuse
cheap relative to retraining.

\subsection{Regime Matching}
Matching is by regime identifier: on a transition to regime $r$, the controller
tests whether $r \in \mathcal{P}$. In the enhanced variant a fingerprint-similarity
gate is additionally applied, so reuse also requires the current window's feature
summary to be close to the fingerprint recorded when the checkpoint was created.
The gate converts the binary question ``has this regime been seen?'' into the
weaker question ``has a sufficiently similar instance of this regime been seen?'',
which allows the mechanism to decline reuse when a regime recurs under visibly
different conditions.

\subsection{Policy Transfer}
On a reuse decision the retrieved ensemble becomes the active policy. Its
soft-voting weights are optionally recalibrated on the most recent buffered window
by measuring the recent accuracy of the random-forest and extra-trees components
and renormalizing their weights. Calibration is bounded to the last 50 buffered
observations and skipped when fewer than 20 are available, so its cost is fixed
and small. When no checkpoint exists, a new policy is trained from a class-anchored
buffer and stored. The buffer always retains a stratified slice of the initial
training prefix, applied identically to every adaptive model in our comparison, to
prevent a refit from collapsing onto a single class when the recent window is
homogeneous.

\subsection{Adaptation Trigger}
Two monitoring rules are available, and their difference is one of the findings of
this work. The \emph{absolute} rule fires when the rolling accuracy of the active
policy falls below a fixed floor. The \emph{relative} rule fires when rolling
accuracy has dropped by more than a fixed margin relative to a decayed reference
built from that same policy's earlier accuracy. The relative rule targets
deterioration rather than poor performance, and Section~\ref{sec:trigger} shows
that this distinction has a sharp consequence: a policy that was never good cannot
be detected by a relative rule, because the reference decays toward the poor level
and the gap the rule looks for never opens.

\subsection{Refresh Mechanism}
A refresh rebuilds the active policy from the recent buffer and replaces the
corresponding checkpoint, so later reuses of that regime retrieve the refreshed
policy. The default refresh is a full refit of the 100-tree ensemble on a
1000-observation buffer. The cost-aware refresh retains the same trigger but
refits a smaller 20-tree ensemble on a 300-observation buffer.
Section~\ref{sec:cost} reports the measurement that motivated this substitution.

\subsection{RAPT-Enhanced}
RAPT-Enhanced adds two mechanisms. \emph{Buffer-blended novelty refitting} refits a
novel regime on a larger recent buffer of 1500 observations rather than 500, and
\emph{selective parity refitting} refits a reused policy from the recent buffer and
replaces its checkpoint when rolling accuracy falls below a parity threshold over a
short window. Parity refitting addresses the stale-reuse failure directly, because
it does not require the regime identifier to change before a retrieved policy can be
corrected.

\subsection{Fallback}
When no checkpoint exists and the similarity gate declines an available one, the
controller trains a fresh policy from the class-anchored buffer and stores it under
the incoming regime identifier. The repository grows by one entry per genuinely
new regime, and the fallback path is exactly the full-retraining behaviour that
RAPT avoids only when it is unnecessary.

\section{Experimental Methodology}
\label{sec:protocol}

\subsection{Datasets}
Four network streams are used, summarised in Table~\ref{tab:datasets}.

\textbf{5G Campus Network QoS.} A processed per-window QoS stream from a 5G campus
testbed, with 19 numerical features per window and a three-class target (GOOD,
DEGRADED, BAD) from the next window's 90th-percentile delay. Regimes are network
scenario labels, and three regimes recur 14 times.

\textbf{UGR'16.} An ISP netflow archive of per-minute traffic aggregates with 134
features, widely used for network intrusion detection evaluation \cite{ugr16}. Its
calibration split contains only blacklisted traffic and no labelled attacks, so the
labelled 30-day block is used. The target is binary (whether any non-blacklisted
attack occurs in the window), and regimes are workday/weekend crossed with four-hour
time-of-day blocks, giving twelve regimes and 168 recurrence events, the strongest
recurrence structure in our evaluation.

\textbf{NordicDat.} Cross-border LTE and 5G predictive-QoS traces released under
CC-BY-4.0 \cite{nordicdat}, with a three-class next-window median-delay target
(thresholds fixed on the training prefix) and mobile-network-operator regimes. This
is the most weakly predictable stream and the one with the fewest transitions.

\textbf{5G NR End-to-End Latency.} A simulated 5G NR end-to-end latency dataset
\cite{nrlatency}, pre-windowed into 499 windows of 500 packets each and spanning
four regimes in the sequence $ABCDACBDAB$, with the same three-class QoS target as
the campus stream. It is used for the natural-drift baseline and for the controlled
covariate-, concept- and recurring-concept-drift experiments.

\subsection{Preprocessing and Stream Construction}
All streams follow the same lightweight pipeline: load, apply the documented target
definition, order by timestamp, drop identifier and constant columns, keep numeric
features, map infinities to missing, drop rows with missing targets, impute with
training-prefix medians, encode categorical targets deterministically, and window.
No principal-component analysis, feature selection, hyper-parameter search or
global normalization is applied. Target thresholds and scaling statistics are
computed on the initial training prefix only.

\subsection{Chronological Ordering and Leakage Control}
Every stream is processed in strict chronological order and never shuffled. The
initial training prefix is 20\% of the windows: 36 windows for the three
cross-dataset streams and 99 for the 5G NR latency stream. The prefix is used for
initial fitting and is never evaluated as test data. Windows are predicted before
their labels are used for any adaptation, giving the prequential order
\textbf{predict $\rightarrow$ evaluate $\rightarrow$ adapt}. Regime annotations are
read from the stream and are not derived from the target.

\subsection{Models and Detectors}
The five primary models share one base architecture and one retraining buffer, so
that differences between them reflect only the adaptation mechanism. The base
model is a heterogeneous soft-voting ensemble of a random forest and an extra trees
classifier, each with 50 trees of maximum depth 7. \emph{Frozen} trains once on the
prefix and never adapts; \emph{Event-Driven} retrains when window accuracy drops
below a fixed threshold; \emph{Full Retraining} rebuilds at every regime
transition; \emph{RAPT} and \emph{RAPT-Enhanced} are the controllers of Section~IV.

ADWIN, Page-Hinkley, EDD and EDMA are evaluated separately as adaptation triggers
driving the same retraining harness, with identical model, buffer, capacity and
minimum retrain interval; only the trigger differs. Parameters are fixed a priori
and not tuned on the test stream (ADWIN $\delta=0.002$; Page-Hinkley with minimum
30 instances and threshold 50; EDD with warning level 2 and drift level 3; EDMA
with $\alpha=0.2$ and $k=2.0$). Because ground-truth drift boundaries are not
available for all streams, we report detected events, adaptation cost and
predictive performance rather than detector precision and recall.

\subsection{Metrics and Statistics}
Predictive performance is reported as macro-F1, accuracy, precision and recall.
Adaptation cost is reported as \emph{adaptation CPU}, the process time spent inside
adaptation routines only, and \emph{runtime}, the total time of the streaming
evaluation including feature construction and prediction. Keeping these separate
matters because a reduction in adaptation time does not translate proportionally
into a reduction in total runtime. We also record retraining events, reuse events,
trees trained and trees reused. All experiments use seeds $[42, 43, 44, 45, 46]$
and report mean $\pm$ standard deviation over seeds. Paired comparisons use the
Wilcoxon signed-rank test with Cohen's $d$. Where pairing is by window the number
of paired observations is 143--146; where it is by seed, $n=5$ and the smallest
attainable two-sided $p$-value is $0.0625$. This ceiling is stated wherever it
binds, and a non-significant result is reported as a failure to establish a
difference rather than as evidence of equivalence. All measurements were taken in a
single process on one machine with \texttt{n\_jobs}$=1$, so timings are comparable
within the study but are not portable absolute costs.

places, so the enhancement produced no measurable change here.

UGR'16 is where the mechanism's assumptions break down, and it is the most
informative result in the table. Frozen recorded the highest macro-F1 at
$0.9690 \pm 0.0029$, consistent with attacks being sparse and temporally
clustered. RAPT recorded $0.8360 \pm 0.0262$, $0.1236$ below Full Retraining
($p = 2.6 \times 10^{-11}$, $d = -0.67$) and the lowest of the five models,
despite 133 reuses. The twelve regimes recur frequently and their identifiers
remain valid across the 30-day block, but the relationship between netflow
features and attack labels does not, so reuse retrieves policies that match the
regime label while no longer matching its label semantics. RAPT-Enhanced, which
parity-refits a degraded reused policy, recovered to $0.9276 \pm 0.0123$, a gain
of $0.0917$ ($p = 5.4 \times 10^{-9}$, $d = 0.56$) and the only statistically
supported improvement of one variant over another in this study.

NordicDat is the weakly predictable stream: Frozen recorded $0.2779 \pm 0.0224$
while adaptation helps, with Full Retraining at $0.4227 \pm 0.0299$ and RAPT at
$0.3818 \pm 0.0184$. RAPT's deficit against Full Retraining is $0.0409$ and not
significant ($p = 0.255$, $d = -0.12$), though RAPT recorded the highest accuracy
here, $0.6172 \pm 0.0127$ against $0.5694 \pm 0.0075$, reflecting a
precision-favouring decision profile rather than better macro-F1. On the 5G NR
latency stream the five models span only $0.0133$ macro-F1, with Full Retraining
leading at $0.9027 \pm 0.0059$ and RAPT lowest at $0.8894 \pm 0.0110$; the
difference is significant on accuracy ($p = 0.0043$) but small in magnitude.

The predictive results do not support a claim that RAPT improves accuracy: on
three of four streams Full Retraining records the highest macro-F1, and RAPT is
below it on all four. The case for the mechanism rests on cost and on the
conditions under which reuse is safe.

\subsection{Computational Cost and Policy Reuse}
Table~\ref{tab:primary} also reports adaptation and total cost, and
Fig.~\ref{fig:cost} shows the adaptation-CPU comparison. The reduction relative to
Full Retraining is consistent in direction across all four streams: $1.4008$ to
$0.2292$\,s on the 5G Campus stream ($-83.6\%$), $23.6318$ to $1.9711$\,s on UGR'16
($-91.7\%$), $2.2034$ to $0.2594$\,s on NordicDat ($-88.2\%$), and $0.7636$ to
$0.3955$\,s on the 5G NR stream ($-48.2\%$). Reductions in total runtime are smaller
in every case ($-44.0\%$, $-82.9\%$, $-40.6\%$, $-14.5\%$), the gap being the
prediction and feature-construction cost that adaptation does not touch. That gap is
largest on the 5G NR stream, where Full Retraining performs only nine retrains.

The event counts explain where the saving comes from. Frozen performs no adaptation
and Event-Driven performs one to eleven retrains depending on the stream, while Full
Retraining retrains at every regime transition (14, 144, 18 and 9). RAPT performs 2,
11, 2 and 3 retrains while retrieving a stored policy 12, 133, 16 and 6 times, and
UGR'16 shows the most extreme ratio at 133 reuses against 11 retrains.

\subsection{Cross-Dataset Behaviour}
Comparing streams isolates which findings depend on the data. The adaptation-cost
reduction and the predominance of reuse over retraining appear on all four, so both
are properties of the mechanism rather than of one stream. The predictive outcome is
not: RAPT-Enhanced improves over base RAPT by $0.0917$ on UGR'16 and by $0.0021$ on
the 5G NR stream, and the two are identical on the 5G Campus stream. The enhancement
matters where regimes recur and their label relationship changes, and is inert
otherwise: UGR'16 has 168 recurrence events across twelve regimes while the 5G
Campus stream has 14 across three.

Reuse frequency alone says nothing about whether a retrieved policy was
appropriate. UGR'16 is the counter-example, where RAPT reuses 133 times and records
the lowest macro-F1 of the five models while RAPT-Enhanced reuses the same 133 times
and records $0.9276$. The two differ not in how often they reuse but in what happens
afterwards, since the enhanced variant re-examines a reused policy and refits it when
recent accuracy falls below the parity threshold. The same reuse frequency therefore
yields a $0.0917$ difference, locating the failure in the reuse decision rather than
in reuse as such. On the 5G Campus stream the two are identical, so the parity refit
never fired and the $0.0455$ gap to Full Retraining persists in both.

The NordicDat results resist a single reading: all models perform poorly in
absolute terms and the ordering of accuracy and macro-F1 inverts, with RAPT
recording the highest accuracy at $0.6172$ while Full Retraining records the highest
macro-F1 at $0.4227$. Whether this inversion is a property of streams with weak
baselines or of this target construction cannot be separated with the present data,
and we do not merge the two into one claim.

\label{sec:ablation}

absolute accuracy floor (RAPT-Floor) fired 7.6 refreshes on average and recorded
$0.9797 \pm 0.0050$ at $0.5865$\,s, above RAPT-Full but with higher seed

$0.9847 \pm 0.0028$ at $2.7168$\,s, the cheap refit retains accuracy while cutting
adaptation CPU by roughly 69\%.

that does not use incremental learning.

\emph{matched} Full Retraining's predictive performance at substantially lower

statistically significant, so no claim of superiority is made; the evidence for
RAPT-Cheap is the cost reduction, not the accuracy difference.

\subsection{Cost Drivers and Trigger Behaviour}
\label{sec:cost}
The cheap refresh was chosen from a direct measurement rather than an assumption
about which factor dominates refit cost. Holding the ensemble fixed and varying one
factor at a time, a 20-tree refit cost approximately 22\,ms and a 100-tree refit
approximately 104\,ms, whereas a 200-observation buffer cost approximately 80\,ms
and a 1000-observation buffer approximately 104\,ms. Tree count therefore dominated
buffer size here, and reducing it from 100 to 20 removed roughly four fifths of the
refit cost, which is what makes a periodic refresh affordable. This is a property of
the evaluated configuration, not a general law: an ensemble whose per-tree or
buffer-processing cost differs would shift the balance.

\label{sec:trigger}
The zero-refresh result for RAPT-Evidence (Section~\ref{sec:ablation}) is a
mechanism-level finding rather than a tuning outcome. A relative trigger asks whether current accuracy has fallen by more
than a margin against a decayed reference built from that policy's own earlier
accuracy. When a regime is persistently poor rather than deteriorating, the
reference decays toward the poor level and the gap the rule tests for never opens,
so the trigger stays silent even though the policy is unfit. RAPT-Floor detected the
same condition against a fixed floor and fired 7.6 refreshes where the relative rule
fired none. The floor is stream-specific: the value used here was selected on the
evaluated stream and would require calibration elsewhere, which is why it is
presented as a mechanism to be tuned rather than a recommended constant.

\subsection{Statistical Analysis}
The paired comparisons support a narrow set of conclusions. On UGR'16, RAPT's
deficit against Full Retraining is significant ($p = 2.6 \times 10^{-11}$,
$d = -0.67$) and RAPT-Enhanced's recovery over RAPT is significant
($p = 5.4 \times 10^{-9}$, $d = 0.56$), both with window-level pairing over 144
observations. On the 5G Campus stream, RAPT's deficits against Full Retraining
($p = 2.6 \times 10^{-4}$, $d = -0.32$) and Event-Driven ($p = 5.7 \times 10^{-3}$)
are significant, and on NordicDat its deficit against Full Retraining is not
($p = 0.255$) while its advantage over Event-Driven is ($p = 1.8 \times 10^{-4}$);
on the 5G NR stream the differences against Full Retraining and Frozen are
significant but small.

One caveat applies throughout: the window-level tests treat windows from one stream
as independent observations, which overstates the effective sample size for claims
intended to generalise beyond the evaluated streams, and the five-seed ablation
comparisons against Full Retraining ($p = 0.3125$ and $p = 0.375$) are limited by
power as well as by effect size, so we report them as non-significant.

\section{Discussion}

The cost result has a straightforward explanation. Retraining at a regime
transition rebuilds the ensemble from a buffer, and that cost scales with tree count
and buffer size. Reuse retrieves an ensemble built earlier and stored by reference,
so its cost is a dictionary lookup plus a bounded weight calibration. Where regimes
recur and their label relationships hold, the retrieved policy is adequate and the
rebuild was unnecessary, so the saving tracks how often regimes recur.

RAPT-Cheap worked for a measurable reason. The refit-cost probe showed tree count
dominating buffer size for this ensemble, so a refresh rebuilding 20 trees on a
300-observation buffer retains enough capacity to correct a policy already close to
the target while costing a fraction of the full refit. The periodic schedule is what
makes it effective on this stream, since a reused policy would otherwise remain
frozen for the whole duration of a recurring regime; neither mechanism alone accounts
for the result.

The incremental mechanism failed, and the diagnostics locate the failure. A
standalone incremental ensemble reached approximately 0.945 macro-F1 against
approximately 0.98 for the batch ensemble, and adding trees did not close the gap
(one tree 0.946, three 0.945, ten 0.941) while cost grew. Blending the
incremental learner into the reused batch policy was also unhelpful, reducing
macro-F1 to approximately 0.963 at weight 0.5 and leaving it at approximately 0.983
at weight 0.3, effectively the batch-only result. The batch ensemble is already close
to ceiling on a 19-feature, three-class problem, leaving little headroom for a second
learner. This is a finding about this stream and problem size, not a general claim
about incremental learning.

The trigger finding generalises more cleanly than the incremental one, because it
follows from the definition of the rule rather than from the data. A relative trigger
compares a policy against its own past, so a policy that was always poor has no
relative drop to detect. This is a structural blind spot rather than a tuning
problem, and it is why the absolute floor was introduced, though that floor needs
per-stream calibration, whereas the relative rule needs no constant but cannot see
the case that matters most for a reused policy.

The cross-dataset results point to recurrence as the condition under which the
mechanism is worth deploying. The cost saving appears wherever reuse occurs, but the
accuracy penalty is concentrated on UGR'16, where regimes recur while their label
relationship does not. That is precisely where a reused policy is stale and where the
parity refit earns its place, converting $0.8360$ into $0.9276$, so policy reuse
should be paired with a check on the reused policy's own recent performance; reuse on
the strength of the regime identifier alone is unsafe when label semantics can drift
independently of it.

The detector comparison shows that the choice between adaptation strategies is a
choice of operating point rather than of winner. The conservative detectors never
fired on these streams and matched the frozen baseline, which is defensible when the
stream is stationary and a failure when it is not, while EDD tracked the drift at the
highest adaptation cost. RAPT sits at the low-cost end among the methods that adapt,
and what distinguishes it is a different operating point: it spends adaptation budget
on retrieval rather than on rebuilding. The conditions for RAPT to be useful follow with some precision. The
stream must provide a usable regime signal, regimes must recur often enough for the
repository to be hit, and the label relationship within a regime must be stable or
monitored so a stale retrieval can be corrected. Without recurrence the mechanism
reduces to retraining with additional bookkeeping, and where label semantics drift
independently of the regime label, reuse without monitoring is worse than not
reusing at all, as UGR'16 shows.

\section{Limitations and Threats to Validity}
\label{sec:limitations}

The evaluation uses five seeds, capping the attainable two-sided $p$-value at
$0.0625$ for seed-level comparisons, so several comparisons with large effect sizes
remain formally non-significant and the ablation results in particular are limited by
power rather than by effect size alone. Window-level tests have more observations but
treat windows from a single stream as independent, overstating the effective sample
size for claims intended to generalise. Three of the four streams are used as
released, but the drift-severity study on the 5G NR latency stream applies controlled
transformations at known points, so its severity response is measured on constructed
drift. Timings come from one environment with parallelism disabled and are
internally comparable but not portable absolute costs.

Further limitations follow from the design. The mechanism depends on regime
recurrence, which is measured rather than assumed; a stream without recurrence would
never exercise the repository, and a genuinely novel regime falls back to retraining
with no advantage over the baseline. The absolute floor used by RAPT-Floor is a fixed
threshold calibrated on the evaluated stream and the 20-tree cheap refit is a
configuration chosen for this ensemble, so both would need re-examination where the
pre- and post-drift difference is larger and a reduced-capacity refit could underfit.
A policy retrieved for a regime whose label relationship has changed is stale, and
only the parity refit in the enhanced variant addresses this. The repository grows
with the number of distinct regimes and is never pruned, and the approach assumes
regime similarity is meaningful, which holds for semantically defined regimes but
would be questionable if the regime signal were noisy. Class imbalance remains a
factor throughout, and on UGR'16 the sparse target is part of why the frozen
baseline is strong there; the class-anchored buffer prevents degenerate
single-class refits but does not remove the imbalance.

\section{Conclusion}

Conventional drift adaptation pays for adaptation by retraining, and where
operating conditions recur that expenditure is partly redundant. RAPT retains the
adaptation policy learned for each regime and retrieves it when the regime returns,
falling back to retraining only when no suitable policy exists and refreshing a
retrieved policy when monitoring shows it has stopped fitting. Across four network
streams the mechanism reduced adaptation CPU relative to full retraining by 48\% to
92\%, with the largest savings where regimes recur most often. The saving did not
come with an accuracy improvement: RAPT recorded lower macro-F1 than full retraining
on all four streams, and where regimes recur but their label relationship changes,
blind reuse was actively harmful, recovering only when a parity-refit mechanism was
added. A cost-aware configuration matched full retraining within measurement noise
($\Delta$macro-F1 $= +0.0022$, $p = 0.31$) at roughly 40\% lower adaptation cost,
the strongest cost-efficiency result in the study.

Two mechanisms were rejected on measured evidence: a relative-degradation trigger
cannot detect a policy that was always poor, because its reference decays to the poor
level, so an absolute floor is needed instead, and an incremental learner was
dominated on both accuracy and cost by a batch configuration without incremental
updating, which the diagnostics attribute to the batch ensemble already being near
ceiling here. Policy reuse is therefore worth deploying where regimes
recur and label semantics are stable or monitored, and the natural extension is to
make the reuse decision adaptive: a controller that estimates whether a retrieved
policy's regime is still semantically the same regime, rather than whether its
identifier matches, would address the failure mode observed on UGR'16 directly.
Whether that estimate can be made without labels is left open.
